Coupon bend test

A strip on two supports, loaded in the middle. Caliper will compare what you read here with what it predicted for the same strip (issue #42).

Each coupon

  1. Measure its width and depth at mid-span, and the span. Weigh it if you can.
  2. Tape or screw two round supports, 1 in pipe or dowel, to sawhorses or a bench so they can't roll. Rest the strip loose on them, so its ends can turn and slide.
  3. Hang the load from a strap over a 1 in wood saddle at the middle, the table's floor gap above a block or pad. If the load touches down before the strip breaks, the numbers are bad: redo it with more gap.
  4. Hang the empty hanger, about 5 lb, and zero the sag reading there. The sag steps count what you add above it.
  5. Go to each of the three load steps. Wait 30 seconds and read the sag. The steps are targets: write what you actually hung.
  6. Back off to the empty hanger. The reading should come back near zero. If it doesn't, something slipped or crushed: redo it.
  7. Where the table says to break it, stop reading sag and add load in small steps from the side, at arm's length. Write the most it held, which includes the hanger and strap, and how it broke. If you run out of load, write "held N, didn't break".
Safety. Eye protection, gloves and closed shoes. Brace or weigh the horses so they can't tip when one end kicks. Near the break, no hand or foot under the load, and never lean over the strip. A snapped strip pivots on its supports and the ends whip up: stand beside it, never in line with either end, and keep people and pets out. Every strap, hanger and scale must be rated above the most you'll hang; luggage scales often stop near 110 lb. With a ratchet strap, keep your face out of its line: a stretched strap can throw the scale. Bagged concrete is 60 to 80 lb, so lift it with care. Nobody stands on a coupon.

Loading: use what you have

  • Sand or gravel in buckets, weighed before you hang them. Two part-full buckets are safer than one full one.
  • Bathroom scale: weigh each bucket on it. Holding a bucket and subtracting yourself is too coarse for 4 and 5 lb steps: use a kitchen or luggage scale there. Or put one support on a board on the scale, note the reading with the strip resting, and as you load take twice the rise. It can't be zeroed with the strip on it, and many digital ones won't follow a slowly rising load, so check yours does first.
  • Weights or bagged concrete on a hanger board.
  • Hanging scale and ratchet strap to a floor anchor: the scale reads the load directly.

Reading the sag: from a reference on the supports

  • Dial indicator clamped to a straight bar that rests on the strip over the supports, tip on the strip beside the saddle. Not on a stand on the floor: that counts crush and the horses' flex.
  • Rule and thread: stretch a thread tight between two pins in the strip, over the supports. Tape a steel rule upright at mid-span, and read it where the thread crosses, at eye level, to 1/64 in or 0.5 mm.
  • No dial for PETG? A rule can't read 1 mm. Skip the sag and just break it: the most it held is still worth having.

Tape a dowel along each inside edge, rest the bar across the dowels, and hang the load down through the gap, between the horses. It has to carry about 100 lb. Sag and break are predictions from the textbook values Caliper uses today, at its default print settings (1.2 mm walls, 20% infill). Real wood often beats them by a lot, and finding out by how much is the point. Most 3/4 ply is 23/32 and most 1/2 ply is 15/32, so expect more sag and a lower break than these; the comparison uses the depth you measure. Three of each kind beats one. PETG may fold instead of snapping. Printed: note layer height, walls, infill and pattern, filament, nozzle temperature. Wood: note knots near the middle, and moisture if you have a meter.

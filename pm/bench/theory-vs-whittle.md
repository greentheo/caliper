Generated by `python -m caliper.coupons --out pm/bench --fea`. Do not edit by hand. The raw numbers are in `theory-vs-whittle.json`.

Each row is one coupon in a three-point bend at its top load step, with the material values Caliper uses today. Each whittle cell is **sag / break load, as multiples of theory**, so 1.00 / 1.00 is a perfect match. A sag under 1 reads too stiff, and a break load over 1 reads too strong.

**Held** is the path a real strength check takes: a pad under each support, every voxel in it pinned. **Alone** takes the supports out: by symmetry, half the beam is a cantilever from mid-span with half the load on its end. **Check's voxels** is the size the check would pick for this coupon, coarser on a long strip because of its voxel limits. **4 across** and **6 across** put that many voxels across the thinnest side, the first being what the check gives a panel whose length doesn't limit it.

## What it says

- **The solver is close when the supports are out of the way and the voxels fit the depth.** At 6 across, alone, sag is 0.87 to 0.96 of theory and the break load 1.07 to 1.16 times theory (leaving out the 2x4 on edge, whose depth isn't a whole number of voxels). whittle's own notes expect this element to read 5 to 20% stiff.
- **Held the way the check holds a part, the strip acts clamped at both ends.** At 4 across, sag is 0.12 to 0.21 of theory and the break load 1.63 to 3.17 times theory. A beam truly clamped at both ends is 0.25 and 2.00. Any support a voxel wide or more stops the end from turning. The short PETG bar reads further off, probably because the pinned pads take up a large share of its span.
- **A depth that isn't a whole number of voxels is rounded up.** The voxels at the check's own size make a strip deeper than it is (`layers` in the JSON), and it reads stiffer and stronger again: sag 0.13 to 0.16 of theory, break load 2.30 to 3.12 times theory, on the 6 coupons long enough that the check picks that coarser size. A real panel short enough to get 4 across exactly doesn't have this.
- **Where that reaches a verdict.** A sheet the check finds resting on other pieces all round, on two opposite edges or on a ring also gets the plate or beam check, which is this theory, and the lower number governs. Anything else has the FEA alone (a sheet on `held()` patches, or on one or three edges, an outline that isn't a rectangle or disc, sticks, printed parts), and where it bends between its holds it can read 1.6 to 3.2 times too strong if the real support lets the end turn. #217 tracks that. Nothing here changes a verdict.
- **The printed rows are one model.** The check has one set of values for a print, whichever way it was laid down, at Caliper's default walls and infill.
- **Across-grain plywood is run here at half E and half strength**, so theory and whittle compare like with like. The real check's FEA has one stiffness and uses the along-grain values. Its plate check takes the across-grain one.
